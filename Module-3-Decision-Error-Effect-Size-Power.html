    Statistics for Psychology · Module 3

    Starting to make sense of stuff

    Decision error, effect size, and power — and how the three of them work together.

    Prof. Lindsay Ouellette

    Before we start

    If this feels a little confusing, don't stress about it. You're going to keep repeating this material until the end of the course, so you'll get plenty of practice sorting it all out.

    Decision error

    When you use the right procedures but still reach the wrong conclusion. Type I and Type II errors.

    Effect size

    Your p value says there's an effect. Effect size says how big it is — and whether anyone should care.

    Power

    The probability your study finds a significant result if your hypothesis is actually true. Decided before you collect data.

    The real point of this module

    It's the interplay between these things that lets you make statistical decisions. No single number decides anything on its own.

    Part 1 · Decision error

    Type I and Type II errors

    A decision error is when a researcher uses the right procedures but makes the wrong decision.

    Type I — false positive

    You got p < .05 and rejected the null. But in reality there was no effect. You claimed something was there when it wasn't.

    Type II — false negative

    You got p ≥ .05 and failed to reject the null. But in reality there was an effect. You missed it.

    The example everyone knows

    Think back to COVID tests. A test says you're positive when you don't have it — that's a false positive, a Type I error. A test says you're negative when you actually do have it — that's a false negative, a Type II error.

    The pregnancy version works the same way: telling an old man he's pregnant is a Type I error. Telling a pregnant woman she isn't is a Type II error.

    Part 1 · Decision error

    The uncomfortable part

    The thing to sit with

    You cannot know that you've made a Type I or Type II error. There is no line in your output that flags it. You ran the correct procedure and got the wrong answer, and everything looks completely normal.

    So what do we do about it?

    The ideal is replication — other scientists repeat your study, or you repeat it yourself, many times. Run the experiment ten times and you may discover that the first result was a Type I or Type II error all along.

    The problem is that a great deal of research never gets replicated. That's the replication crisis, and it's the subject of one of this week's readings.

    Not just psychology

    Cognitive neuroscience has the same problem, and arguably worse. Those samples are often tiny — you're basing conclusions on a handful of people, because each one has to go in an fMRI machine. Running that study with 100 participants would be enormous.

    Where the real defense is

    Experimental design. Statistics is a small part of research — it's the running-the-numbers part. Your design has to be very good to minimise Type I and Type II errors in the first place.

    Research design is properly a different course — at Temple, that's Critical Thinking in Psychology. If you've taken it, this is where it connects.

    Part 2 · Effect size

    Significance only tells you so much

    A cautionary tale

    “This study says massage improves sleep compared to the control group!”

    “That's awesome! How much longer did the massage group sleep?”

    “…47 seconds per night.”

    Assume that study had p < .05. It's statistically significant. It's also 47 seconds. Would you book a massage for that?

    The distinction

    The p value tells you the result should convince you that there is an effect. It does not tell you how big that effect is. Those are two different questions, and you need both.

    Throughout this course, “significance” and “p value” mean the same thing and get used interchangeably.

    Part 2 · Effect size

    Cohen's d

    In psychology, the standard metric for effect size is Cohen's d. It measures the difference between two group means — or equivalently, how much the two groups overlap.

    Small

    d = .2

    Medium

    d = .5

    Large

    d = .8

    Cohen's d used to be treated as a 0-to-1 scale. It's since been extended — some researchers now go up to 2.0, where d = 2 is a huge effect.

    Picture it

    Say you're testing a depression medication. One group gets the medication, one is a control. If the medication works, you want a big difference between the two group means — and therefore not much overlap between the two distributions.

    Part 3 · Power

    Statistical power

    Definition

    Statistical power is the probability that the study will produce a statistically significant result if the research hypothesis is true.

    What it's for

    Deciding how many participants you need

    This happens before you collect any data. It's part of research design, not analysis.

    Making sense of your results

    Helps you interpret findings that aren't significant — or findings that are statistically significant but not practically significant.

    An example of that second case: a significant p value, under .05, but an effect size of d = 0.1. Statistically real, practically almost nothing.

    Why you care

    Good statistical power helps you avoid Type I and Type II errors. That's the connection back to where this module started — the three topics aren't separate.

    What determines power?

    1. Effect size — how big an effect your research hypothesis predicts
    2. Sample size — how many participants are in the study
    The convention

    Generally you want 80% power — an 8-out-of-10 chance the study produces a statistically significant result if the research hypothesis is true.

    How you'd actually get your effect size estimate

    You don't know your effect size before you run the study — so you borrow one. Build a spreadsheet of a handful of published experiments similar to what you want to do. Record each one's sample size, effect size, and p value. Average them. Feed that into the power software.

    That gives you the minimum number of participants you need to run your study well.

    Part 5 · t-tests

    What is a t-test?

    Definition

    A statistical test used to compare the means of two groups of data.

    That's it. Everything else — the flavours, the notation, the reporting conventions — is detail hanging off that one idea. You have two means, and you want to know whether the difference between them is bigger than you'd expect by chance.

    Part 5 · t-tests

    Three kinds of t-test

    Which one you use depends on what you're comparing.

    One sample t-test

    Compares your sample mean against a fixed or hypothetical population value.

    Is this classroom's average score different from the national average of 100?

    Independent two-sample t-test

    Compares two unrelated groups.

    aka between group / between subjects · unpaired

    Paired sample t-test

    Compares the same group at two points — before and after.

    aka within group / within subjects · dependent sample

    The question that decides it

    Are the two sets of scores coming from the same people? If yes, it's paired. If they're different people, it's independent. If there's only one group and you're comparing to a known number, it's one-sample.

    Part 5 · t-tests

    Reporting a t-test

    Values you must report

    Means (M) and standard deviations (SD) for each group · the t value · degrees of freedom, in parentheses next to t · and the significance level (p).

    Two examples

    Significant

    Women (M = 3.66, SD = .40) reported significantly higher levels of happiness than men (M = 3.20, SD = .32), t(1) = 5.44, p < .05.

    Not significant

    Men (M = 4.05, SD = .50) and women (M = 4.11, SD = .55) did not differ significantly on levels of extraversion, t(1) = 1.03, p = n.s.

    n.s. means “not significant.” Notice the non-significant version still reports every value — you don't get to leave the numbers out because the result didn't go your way.

    Keep this

    This reporting format is in your stats toolbox module. You'll use it for every write-up from here on.

    Part 5 · t-tests

    A worked example

    Chick weight by diet — an independent samples t-test, written up properly.

    Anatomy of a resultClick any highlighted piece
    Click any highlighted part of the sentence to see what it's doing.

    Why is t negative?

    Because of the order the groups went into the test — diet 1 first. A negative t here just means the second group scored higher. It is not a bad result, and the sign carries no meaning about quality.

    The CI as a cross-check

    The 95% confidence interval is [−33.89, −6.06]. It does not contain zero — which is another way of seeing the same thing p < .05 told you. If a CI straddles zero, the result isn't significant.

    From result to discussion

    Here's that same study written up as a discussion paragraph. Watch what it adds — it doesn't repeat the numbers, it makes meaning out of them.

    An independent samples t-test was conducted to determine whether there was a significant difference in the weights of chicks based on their diet, specifically comparing diet 1 and diet 2. The results revealed that chicks on diet 2 had a significantly higher mean weight compared to chicks on diet 1. This finding suggests that diet 2 is associated with greater weight in chicks compared to diet 1. These results could inform dietary recommendations for optimizing chick growth.

    The higher average weight and larger standard deviation for chicks on diet 2 may suggest not only a higher mean weight but also greater variability in weight outcomes. Further research could explore the specific components of diet 2 that contribute to increased weight and investigate whether similar results are observed across different populations of chicks or under varying environmental conditions. Additionally, understanding the nutritional differences between the two diets could provide insights into specific dietary requirements for optimal chick development.

    Look at what it's doing

    That paragraph follows the same six moves you're about to learn: what was found, what it suggests, why it matters, a limitation (the larger SD — more variability), and future research. Keep it in view for the next section.

    Part 5 · Writing it up

    Writing your discussion

    The discussion is where you put on your scientist cap and give your opinion.

    What a discussion actually is

    This is what I love about science: it's about giving opinions based on the evidence — from your study, from your descriptive statistics — and making meaning out of it.

    It's also, to me, the hardest part of statistics. Harder than the inferential machinery. How do we make meaning from these numbers?

    Part 5 · Writing it up

    Have confidence to have an opinion

    The whole point

    People like giving their opinions. A discussion section is one of the rare places in academic writing where you're supposed to — as long as the evidence is underneath it.

    An opinion grounded in evidence

    “The medium effect size suggests this intervention may be clinically useful, though the small sample means this should be treated as preliminary.”

    Not an opinion — just hedging

    “The results were mixed and further research is needed to determine whether the intervention is effective.”

    The difference is that the first one says something. It commits, and it shows you why. The second could be pasted into any paper ever written.
